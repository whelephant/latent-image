There is a moment in the darkroom that has no equivalent in daily life. The paper
has been exposed; the image is already there, complete, latent in the emulsion —
and yet you cannot see it. You slide the sheet into the developer and wait, and the
picture rises out of the white like something remembering itself. It is the closest
I have come to watching time run backward.

I have been thinking about that interval — the gap between the exposure and the
print — because it is the part of photography that digital tools have most
efficiently abolished, and the part I most want to keep.

## What the interval was for

The latency was never only a technical inconvenience. It was a discipline. You shot
on faith, not on confirmation. You could not chimp the back of the camera and decide,
in the half-second after the shutter, whether the picture had "worked." You had to
hold the possibility of the image in your head for hours, sometimes days, and that
holding changed what you noticed next.

When everything resolves instantly, attention gets cheap. We take more and look less.
The frame on the screen is so immediately *legible* that we mistake legibility for
seeing. But a photograph is not an answer you check; it is a claim you make about what
deserved to be kept.

## Writing has the same chemistry

The essay, it turns out, develops on a similar curve. The first draft is the exposure:
fast, instinctive, mostly wrong in ways you cannot yet perceive. The temptation is to
publish at the speed of typing — to chimp the prose. But the sentence that looked sharp
in the moment is usually just *bright*, not *focused*, and the difference only appears
after the interval.

So I have started treating drafts like undeveloped film. I expose them quickly and then
deliberately refuse to look. What returns after a few days is not the same text. The
grain has settled. I can finally tell which lines carry the picture and which were only
exposure noise.

## Developing slowly

I do not think slowness is a virtue in itself. Plenty of slow work is merely late. What
I am defending is the *interval* — the structural pause that lets a first impression
correct itself before it hardens into a published one.

The darkroom enforced that pause with chemistry. Now I have to enforce it with habit,
which is harder, because nothing stops me from looking. Everything is always already
developed. The discipline is to behave as if it weren't — to keep, by choice, the gap
the technology removed.

That is the whole method, such as it is. Expose with instinct. Wait past the urge to
confirm. Then come back and let the image tell you, slowly, what it was always going to
be.
